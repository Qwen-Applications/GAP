**Question:** Moving only south and east along the line segments, how many paths are there from $A$ to $B$?\n\nPut your final answer within \\boxed{}." 

**Answer:**

<think>
The problem asks for the number of paths from point A (top-left) to point B (bottom-right), moving only east (right) and south (down) along the grid lines. The grid is mostly complete, but there are two missing horizontal line segments that block certain paths.

To reason clearly, we should construct an auxiliary image that represents the grid as a coordinate-based lattice. This auxiliary image should mark A, B, and the endpoints of the two missing horizontal segments so that the blocked-path counting can be organized systematically and the Principle of Inclusion–Exclusion can be applied cleanly.
<aux_image>
<parser>
Draw a rectangular grid consisting of 9 columns (east direction) and 3 rows (south direction), forming a lattice of horizontal and vertical line segments.

Mark:
- Point A at the top-left corner of the grid.
- Point B at the bottom-right corner of the grid.

Indicate two missing horizontal segments:
- The first missing segment lies between the grid points (4,1) and (5,1). Mark the left endpoint as C and the right endpoint as C′.
- The second missing segment lies between the grid points (4,2) and (5,2). Mark the left endpoint as D and the right endpoint as D′.

Label coordinates implicitly by showing that:
- Moving right increases the east coordinate.
- Moving down increases the south coordinate.

Ensure the missing segments are clearly absent, while all other grid lines are intact.
</parser>

We now count paths step by step.

First, ignore the missing segments and count all possible paths on the complete grid. To go from A to B, one must make 9 east moves and 3 south moves, for a total of 12 moves. The number of such paths is:
\[
\binom{12}{3} = 220.
\]

Next, subtract the paths that pass through the first missing segment \(S_1\), which connects C=(4,1) to C′=(5,1).
- Paths from A to C require 4 east and 1 south move: \(\binom{5}{1} = 5\).
- From C to C′ there is exactly 1 way.
- Paths from C′ to B require 4 east and 2 south moves: \(\binom{6}{2} = 15\).
Thus, paths using \(S_1\): \(5 \times 1 \times 15 = 75\).

Then, subtract the paths that pass through the second missing segment \(S_2\), which connects D=(4,2) to D′=(5,2).
- Paths from A to D require 4 east and 2 south moves: \(\binom{6}{2} = 15\).
- From D to D′ there is exactly 1 way.
- Paths from D′ to B require 4 east and 1 south move: \(\binom{5}{1} = 5\).
Thus, paths using \(S_2\): \(15 \times 1 \times 5 = 75\).

No path can pass through both missing segments, since doing so would require moving west or north, which is not allowed. Therefore, there is no overlap to add back.

Finally, applying inclusion–exclusion:
\[
220 - 75 - 75 = 70.
\]

Hence, the number of valid paths from A to B is 70.
</think>
<answer>
The auxiliary grid construction lets us count paths systematically. If no segments were missing, traveling from \(A\) to \(B\) would require 9 east moves and 3 south moves, so the total number of monotone paths would be \(\binom{12}{3} = 220\). We then subtract the paths that are forced to use the first missing horizontal segment and the paths that are forced to use the second missing horizontal segment; each of those sets contains 75 paths. Because a valid path moving only east and south cannot pass through both missing segments, there is no overlap to add back. Thus the final count is \(220 - 75 - 75 = 70\), so the number of valid paths is \(\boxed{70}\).
</answer>

# Example 3

**Question:** A right pyramid has a square base that measures 10 cm on each side. Its peak is 12 cm above the center of its base. What is the sum of the lengths of the pyramid's eight edges? Express your answer to the nearest whole number.\nPut your final answer within \\boxed{}.
**Answer:**
<think>
We are given a **right square pyramid**. To compute the sum of the lengths of its eight edges, we must clearly distinguish between:
- the four base edges (all known), and
- the four slant edges (unknown, but equal by symmetry).

To support the reasoning, we should first construct an auxiliary image showing the full right square pyramid, including the square base, the apex, and the vertical height from the apex to the center of the base. This makes the known geometric structure explicit and separates the four base edges from the four equal slant edges.
<aux_image>
<parser>
Draw a right square pyramid.

- Draw a square base lying horizontally.
- Label each side of the square as 10 cm.
- Mark the center of the square clearly.

- Draw the apex directly above the center of the base.
- Draw a vertical dashed line from the apex straight down to the center of the square.
- Label this vertical segment as 12 cm (the height).

Edges:
- Draw four slanted edges connecting the apex to each vertex of the square base.
- Draw four base edges forming the square.

Ensure:
- The pyramid is symmetric.
- The vertical height is perpendicular to the base.
- All edges are clearly visible.
</parser>

From the diagram, we first compute the total length of the base edges.  
The square base has 4 edges, each of length 10 cm:
\[
4 \times 10 = 40 \text{ cm}.
\]

Next, we focus on finding the length of a slant edge. To do this cleanly, we should construct another auxiliary image that isolates the right triangle formed by:
- the pyramid height,
- the segment from the center of the base to a vertex,
- and the slant edge itself.
<aux_image>
<parser>
Draw a right triangle extracted from the pyramid.

Points:
- One point represents the apex.
- One point represents the center of the square base.
- One point represents a vertex of the square base.

Segments:
- Draw a vertical segment from the apex to the base center and label it 12 cm.
- Draw a horizontal segment from the base center to a base vertex.
- Label this horizontal segment as 5√2 cm.
- Draw the hypotenuse from the apex to the base vertex and label it as s (the slant edge).

Angles:
- Mark a right angle at the base center between the vertical and horizontal segments.
</parser>

We now compute the missing lengths.

The diagonal of the square base is:
\[
\sqrt{10^2 + 10^2} = 10\sqrt{2}.
\]
The distance from the center of the square to a vertex is half the diagonal:
\[
\frac{10\sqrt{2}}{2} = 5\sqrt{2}.
\]

Using the Pythagorean theorem on the right triangle:
\[
s^2 = 12^2 + (5\sqrt{2})^2 = 144 + 50 = 194,
\]
so
\[
s = \sqrt{194} \approx 13.93 \text{ cm}.
\]

There are four identical slant edges:
\[
4 \times 13.93 \approx 55.71 \text{ cm}.
\]

Finally, summing all eight edges:
\[
40 + 55.71 \approx 95.71 \text{ cm}.
\]

Rounded to the nearest whole number, the sum of the lengths of the pyramid’s eight edges is 96.
</think>
<answer>
The square base contributes four edges of length 10 cm, so the total length of the base edges is \(4 \times 10 = 40\) cm. To find a slant edge, we use the right triangle formed by the pyramid’s height, the segment from the center of the base to a vertex, and the slant edge itself. Since the base is a 10-by-10 square, its diagonal is \(10\sqrt{2}\), so the distance from the center to a vertex is half of that, namely \(5\sqrt{2}\). Applying the Pythagorean theorem gives \(s^2 = 12^2 + (5\sqrt{2})^2 = 194\), so \(s = \sqrt{194} \approx 13.93\) cm. The four slant edges therefore contribute about \(4 \times 13.93 = 55.71\) cm, and the sum of all eight edges is approximately \(40 + 55.71 = 95.71\) cm. Rounded to the nearest whole number, the total is \(\boxed{96}\).
</answer>
